ANATOMY OF A PRICE GEMBA 402 · Santa Monica Real Estate Case
GEMBA 402 · Regression & SHAP · Exploratory study

What actually sets the price of a Santa Monica home?

299 home sales closed in Santa Monica during 2021 — $1.21 billion of transacted value, from a $1.2M bungalow to a $48.7M compound. Five variables, four regressions, and a lesson in what happens when your predictors measure the same thing twice.

Scroll to move through the analysis — every chart is live, hover any mark. Chapters 3–6 work through the case questions in order. Built from Santa Monica 2022 data 1.xlsx.
Chapter 1

First, the market as it actually traded

Before any model, look at the raw distribution. Nearly every conclusion downstream is shaped by the fact that this market is not symmetric — it has a long, thin, extremely expensive tail.

299 closings

Every sale, one dot

Each circle is a single closed transaction between 6 January and 31 December 2021. 298 are single-family residences; one is a condo. 295 sit inside Santa Monica proper, with four just over the line in Venice, Mar Vista and Pacific Palisades.

No missing values, no duplicate addresses — an unusually clean 299 rows.

Distribution

Order them by price and the market splits in two

The median home traded at $3.30M. The average traded at $4.05M — 23% higher. When the mean runs that far ahead of the median, a handful of sales are carrying the arithmetic.

The middle 50% of the market sits between $2.34M and $4.79M. Everything above that is tail.

Shape

A right skew of 7.8

Nearly two-thirds of the market — 64% — clears under $4M. Then the histogram thins out into a long runway of bins holding one or two sales apiece.

This matters for the modelling: least squares assumes roughly symmetric, constant-variance errors, and this target is neither. Hold that thought — it comes back in Chapter 6.

The outlier

609 E Channel Rd — $48.7M

14,000 sq ft of interior on a 3.35-acre lot, built 1932, 13 beds, 12.5 baths. It sold on 19 April 2021 for 14.7× the median and 3.8× the next-highest sale in the file.

One row out of 299 — and it accounts for 4.0% of the entire year's transacted dollars. We keep it. It is real market signal, and watching the models handle it is the most informative thing in this dataset.

Chapter 2

The economics before the regression

The case asks you to think about the economic relationships before running multiple regressions. So: which of these five variables measure genuinely different things, and which are the same thing wearing a different label?

Interior size

Square footage: r = 0.77

The obvious driver. A single-variable fit prices interior space at roughly $1,428 per square foot — but the cloud fans out badly above 5,000 sq ft, the first hint that one variable is not going to be enough.

Land

Lot size correlates harder: r = 0.85

On raw correlation, land beats the house standing on it. That is a coastal Los Angeles signature: the dirt is the appreciating asset and the structure is depreciating improvement on top of it.

Critically, lot size is only 0.48 correlated with interior size. These two really are separate economic quantities — which is why both survive every specification below.

The correlation matrix — read this before Chapter 3

BATHS and SQFT are 0.92 correlated

Bedrooms and bathrooms look like strong predictors on their own (r = 0.69 and 0.67). They are not independent signals: BATHS ↔ SQFT = 0.92 and BEDS ↔ SQFT = 0.85. Bigger houses have more bathrooms. That is all those correlations are saying.

Predict this now, before running anything: put SQFT and BATHS in the same regression and there will be almost no independent variation left in BATHS to price.

All five, against price

And AGE runs the wrong way

Age correlates −0.21 with price — older homes sell for less. It also correlates −0.56 with square footage: older homes are smaller. Homes under 20 years old average 5,227 sq ft; homes over 95 average 2,492 sq ft.

Keep both facts in view. In Chapter 5 the sign on AGE flips, and this is why.

Chapter 3 · Case question 1

The simple regression

SALE PRICE regressed on SQFT alone. One predictor, 299 observations, and a coefficient that will not survive contact with the rest of the model.

Q1 · Output

$1,428 per square foot — and a meaningless intercept

The slope is 1,428.40 with a standard error of 68.05, so t = 20.99 and p < 0.0001. A 95% interval on the slope runs $1,294 to $1,562. Square footage is unambiguously priced.

The intercept is −$338,044 with p = 0.167 — not significant, and not interpretable anyway. It is the model's guess for a house with zero square feet, which is an extrapolation far outside the data. Report it; do not explain it.

Q1 · How good is it, really

R² = 0.597, and a typical miss of $2.18M

Square footage alone explains 59.7% of the variation in price. That sounds respectable until you read the standard error of the regression: $2,184,312.

On a median home of $3.30M, the model's typical error is roughly two-thirds of the house. Statistically significant and commercially useless are not the same thing — this model is the first and the second.

Chapter 4 · Case question 2

The full model — and what happens to BEDS and BATHS

Adding AGE, BATHS, BEDS and LOT SIZE lifts R² from 0.597 to 0.902. Almost all of that gain is LOT SIZE. The two variables you would expect to matter most do something strange.

Q2 · What you notice

BATHS is worth nothing. BEDS is worth negative $209,000.

BATHS: −$9,691, p = 0.916. Statistically indistinguishable from zero. Its standard error ($91,583) is 9.4 times the size of the coefficient itself.

BEDS: −$208,812, p = 0.029. Negative, and significant under classical standard errors. Taken at face value, adding a bedroom destroys a fifth of a million dollars of value.

Neither result means what it appears to mean — and the second one does not survive a robustness check either. Chapter 5 ends on that.

Q2 · What explains it

Multicollinearity — the variables are measuring each other

BATHS correlates 0.92 with SQFT, giving it a variance inflation factor of 7.89. Its standard error is roughly 2.8× what it would be if BATHS were uncorrelated with everything else. Once square footage is in the model, there is almost no independent variation in bathroom count left to price — so the estimate is imprecise, and imprecise estimates land near zero.

BEDS is the more interesting case. Its coefficient is a conditional effect: holding square footage and lot size fixed, one more bedroom means the same house partitioned into more, smaller rooms. The market genuinely discounts that. The number is real — it just does not answer "what is a bedroom worth," which is what people read it as.

Note too that SQFT's coefficient fell from $1,428 to $1,084. In Q1 it was standing in for land value; now LOT SIZE takes that share back.

Chapter 5 · Case question 3

Drop BEDS and BATHS — and watch AGE

Removing both costs almost nothing: R² falls from 0.9024 to 0.9005, two-tenths of one percent. They were carrying essentially no unique information. But AGE changes.

Q3 · What you notice

AGE gets stronger, not weaker

The coefficient rises from $5,951 to $6,251 per year and its p-value improves from 0.0175 to 0.0120. Dropping two predictors made a third one sharper.

The mechanism is the same multicollinearity, viewed from the other side. AGE correlates −0.58 with BATHS and −0.45 with BEDS — older Santa Monica homes have fewer bathrooms. While those variables were in the model they were absorbing part of the age effect. Remove them and it returns to AGE, with a smaller standard error.

Q3 · The deeper point

The sign on AGE is positive. Its correlation with price is negative.

Raw, age and price move opposite ways (r = −0.21). In the regression, each additional year of age adds +$6,251. Both are correct.

Older homes are smaller — 2,492 sq ft on average at 95 years or more, against 5,227 sq ft under 20. Size drives price, so unconditionally old reads as cheap. Holding size and lot fixed, a pre-war house on an established Santa Monica block is worth more than a comparable new build.

AGE is not being paid for age. It is proxying for location and lot pedigree — the variable this dataset never measures. That is the honest reading, and it is the single most important caveat in the whole analysis.

Q1 → Q4 · Coefficient trajectory

Watch each coefficient move as the specification changes

SQFT walks down from $1,428 → $1,084 → $957 → $872 as more of the price is correctly attributed elsewhere. LOT SIZE is rock-steady at $235–$245 in every model that contains it — the sign of a variable measuring something genuinely its own.

This chart is the case in one picture: a coefficient is not a property of a variable. It is a property of a variable and the company it keeps.

A check the case does not ask for

Now re-run it with robust standard errors

Every p-value quoted so far assumes constant error variance. Chapter 1 showed that assumption is false here — Breusch-Pagan rejects it in all four specifications (p from 9×10−11 in Q1 to 0.0003 in Q4), and the errors fan out with price.

Refit with HC3 heteroskedasticity-consistent errors and the coefficients do not move at all — but the inference does. SQFT and LOT SIZE stay significant past p < 0.001 everywhere. AGE goes from p = 0.012 to p = 0.091, and BEDS from 0.029 to 0.075. Both counter-intuitive findings fall out of the 5% band.

That does not make them false. The direction of both is corroborated independently by the tree ensemble in Chapters 7 and 8. It makes them suggestive rather than established on 299 rows — which is exactly how they should be reported to anyone about to spend money on them.

Chapter 6 · Case question 4

The final model: two variables, and what it can actually promise

Drop AGE as well and you are left with the specification the case wants — interior size and land. R² 0.8983, adjusted 0.8976, F = 1,307.7. Losing three predictors cost four-tenths of one percent of explanatory power.

Q4a · The model

Price = −593,545 + 872.49 × SQFT + 245.14 × LOT SIZE

SQFT = $872.49. Holding lot size constant, one additional square foot of interior space adds $872 to the expected sale price. t = 22.34.

LOT SIZE = $245.14. Holding interior size constant, one additional square foot of land adds $245. t = 29.60 — the strongest t-statistic in the model.

The intercept, −$593,545, has no economic meaning: it describes a house of zero square feet on a lot of zero square feet. It exists to position the plane, not to be interpreted.

Q4b · Price per square foot

$872.49, with a 95% interval of $795.62 to $949.37

The estimate is the SQFT coefficient itself; the interval is 872.49 ± 1.968 × 39.06. A ±$77 band, or about 9% of the estimate — tight, on 299 observations.

Note this is not the $1,303 median price-per-square-foot you get by dividing sale price by floor area. That naive figure bundles the land into the building. $872 is the marginal value of interior space with land held constant — a genuinely different quantity, and the one a developer adding square footage actually needs.

Q4c · Standard error of the regression

$1,099,424

Computed as √(SSR / (n − k − 1)) with n = 299, k = 2, so 296 degrees of freedom. It is the typical size of a residual — the model's own estimate of how far a given sale will sit from the fitted plane.

Down from $2,184,312 in the simple model: adding land halved the typical error. It is still 33% of a median home, and that number is what drives everything on the next screen.

Q4d · The prediction

$2,551,624 — and an interval you have to be honest about

A 2,200 sq ft house on a 5,000 sq ft lot: −593,545 + 872.49(2,200) + 245.14(5,000) = $2,551,624.

First, the trap: the question also specifies 15 years old, 3 bedrooms, 2 bathrooms. This model contains none of those variables, so none of them change the answer. That is the point of having dropped them.

The 95% confidence interval for the mean of such houses is $2.41M – $2.69M — narrow, because the average is well estimated. The 95% prediction interval for one specific house is $383,467 – $4,719,781. That is ±$2.17M on a $2.55M prediction.

Q4d · Sanity check against the market

Ten real comparable sales say $2.80M

Filtering the data to homes of 1,900–2,500 sq ft on lots of 4,200–6,000 sq ft returns 10 actual 2021 sales, median $2.80M, spanning $2.05M to $4.50M.

The model's $2.55M sits about 9% below that median, and the comps' own spread is nearly $2.5M wide. The prediction interval is not the model being modest — it is the model being accurate about a market where two houses of the same size trade $2.4M apart depending on which block they are on.

Chapter 7 · Beyond the case

SHAP: attribution, one house at a time

The case model tells you what a square foot is worth on average. It cannot tell you why this house sold for what it did. For that we fit a gradient-boosted tree ensemble on an extended feature set and decompose every individual prediction with SHAP — exact per-house, per-attribute dollar contributions that sum to the prediction with nothing left over.

The starting point

Every explanation begins at $3.97M

The base value is the reference point every SHAP explanation starts from — the model's expected output before it knows anything about a specific house. The seven feature contributions, added to this number, land exactly on that house's prediction.

Contributions in red push price up; blue pushes it down.

One house, decomposed

Follow the money from $3.97M to the prediction

The chart opens on 258 21st Pl — 4,493 sq ft, built 1927, sold for $5.5M. Interior size adds +$816k over the base. The 1927 vintage adds another +$522k. The fifth bedroom takes −$98k back off. Model lands at $5.27M against an actual $5.50M.

Use the selector to price any of the 299. This is the line-item view a pricing desk needs — and it is exactly what a single regression coefficient cannot give you.

All 299 × all 7

The beeswarm: every contribution in the dataset

One dot per house per feature — 2,093 in total. Horizontal position is the dollar contribution; colour is the feature's own value, low to high.

Read the colour gradient left-to-right. Where blue sits left and red sits right, more of that attribute means more money. Age breaks the pattern — the same reversal Chapter 5 found, recovered independently by a completely different model class.

Global importance

Interior size is half the model

Averaging absolute contribution across all 299 homes: square footage moves the prediction by $1.12M on average — 49.3% of all attributed movement. Lot size takes another 21.0%.

The two size measures together account for 70.3% of everything the model does — which is a machine-learning restatement of the case's own conclusion that SQFT and LOT SIZE are the model.

Chapter 8

The shape of each effect

A regression coefficient is one number for the whole range of a variable. Dependence plots show what the effect actually does across that range — where it saturates, where it reverses, and what it depends on.

Dependence · interior size

Square footage pays, then pays faster

Below the 2,639 sq ft median, interior size subtracts from the base value — about −$720k in the 2,000–2,639 band. It crosses zero at roughly 3,100 sq ft, then accelerates: +$2.2M for homes of 5,000–7,000 sq ft and +$5.4M above that.

The linear model's single $872 slope is an average across that entire curve. Convexity is precisely what a straight line has to throw away.

Dependence · age — the paradox, again

Old houses correlate with cheap. Old houses price expensive.

Raw correlation between age and price is −0.21. The SHAP contribution for age runs positively with age (+0.55). Homes of 95 years or older average +$362k; homes under 75 average −$139k.

Same finding as Chapter 5, reached without any linear assumption. When two model families with nothing in common agree on a counter-intuitive sign, the effect is in the data, not in the method.

Pairwise interaction strength

Land × vintage is the strongest pairing

Decomposing the SHAP values into main effects and pairwise interactions, the largest is lot size × age, at $103k of average absolute movement per house (the φij + φji total), followed by interior size × lot coverage.

Big old parcels are a distinct market segment, priced differently from big new ones — exactly the structure an additive regression cannot represent, no matter which variables you feed it.

Chapter 9

Where the sophisticated model loses

The tree ensemble captures curvature and interaction the regression cannot. It also gets beaten badly on held-out data — for a reason that has nothing to do with tuning, and everything to do with one row.

5-fold cross-validation

Out-of-fold predictions, both models

Through the body of the market the two are near-identical — median absolute error of $489k for the tree ensemble against $497k for the extended regression. On typical homes you would not choose between them on accuracy.

The extrapolation ceiling

The tree model prices a $48.7M house at $12.1M

A decision tree predicts by averaging training rows inside a leaf. It cannot output a value outside the range it was trained on. Hold out 609 E Channel Rd and the model has never seen anything above $12.9M — so $12.1M is the ceiling.

The regression, extrapolating along a plane, predicts $44.2M for the same unseen house. It is off by $4.4M — a rounding error next to being off by $36.6M.

The scorecard

R² 0.897 versus 0.493 — decided by one row

Because R² is squared-error based, that single miss is most of the gap. Drop the trophy sale from the scoring and the models converge to 0.774 and 0.707.

The honest conclusion: on 299 rows with a near-linear price surface, the regression is the better predictor and SHAP is the better explanation. Use the tree ensemble to learn the shape of the market; use the linear model to quote a number on a trophy property.

Case question 5 · Augmenting the analysis

What is missing — and what to add first

Two variables explain 90% of price variance. The remaining 10% is worth $1.1M per house, and almost all of it is geography the dataset never records.

The single highest-value addition: sub-city location

There is no structured geography in the file below the city name — a street address, but no coordinates, no neighbourhood, no school zone. In a city where a block north of Montana Avenue and a block south of the 10 differ by millions at identical square footage, that is the dominant source of unexplained variance — and it is why AGE reads as a premium rather than the depreciation it physically is. Latitude/longitude, distance to the beach, and school attendance zone would together do more for this model than any modelling technique applied to the current seven columns.

Q5 · A city where the unusual variables dominate

Santa Monica itself already has two variables no generic US model would carry. Coastal Commission jurisdiction caps what can be built west of the coastal zone boundary, so two identical lots can have entirely different buildable envelopes. And since March 2023, Measure GS imposes a 5.6% transfer tax on the entire value of any sale at $8M or more — a $48.7M trade like 609 E Channel Rd would now carry roughly $2.7M of transfer tax, which reprices the whole above-$8M segment. Neither exists in the 2021 data.

Singapore, for contrast, is a market where the conventional variables are almost secondary. Remaining lease tenure is decisive — a 99-year leasehold flat with 45 years left and a freehold unit of identical size are different assets, and lease decay steepens non-linearly past the 40-year mark. HDB versus private is a hard regime boundary with its own eligibility rules. Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty tier — which depends on the buyer's residency and property count, not the property — moves effective price by double digits. Distance to an MRT station, and which line, outweighs floor area in many districts. Primary-school 1km ballot radius capitalises directly into price. And en-bloc potential means an ageing, unattractive block can trade at a premium to a newer one because the land underneath is redevelopable. A hedonic model built on beds, baths and floor area would miss nearly all of it.

Method notes

Case models. statsmodels OLS, classical standard errors, matching standard spreadsheet regression output. Four specifications: SQFT; +AGE/BATHS/BEDS/LOT SIZE; less BEDS/BATHS; less AGE.
Prediction intervals. Confidence interval for the conditional mean and prediction interval for a single observation, both at 95%, t with 296 df.
Extended model (Ch. 7–9). Adds LOT_COVERAGE (SQFT ÷ LOT SIZE) and SALE_MONTH. YEAR_BUILT is dropped throughout — it is a perfect linear inverse of AGE (r = −1.000).
Tree model. XGBoost, 400 trees, depth 3, learning rate 0.05, subsample 0.8. A 48-configuration sweep (depth 2–6, 200–1,000 trees, learning rate 0.02–0.3) returns out-of-fold R² between 0.44 and 0.50 — the ceiling is structural, not a tuning failure.
SHAP. Exact TreeSHAP on the full-data fit, plus pairwise SHAP interaction values.
Validation. 5-fold, shuffled, seed 42. R², MAE and median AE are out-of-fold unless labelled in-sample.
Robustness. The case figures use classical standard errors, matching standard spreadsheet regression output. Every specification was also refit with HC3 heteroskedasticity-consistent errors: SQFT and LOT SIZE remain significant at p < 0.001 throughout, while AGE (0.012 → 0.091 in the Q3 model) and BEDS (0.029 → 0.075 in the Q2 model) drop out of the 5% band. Chapter 5's last screen reports this in full.
Diagnostics. Breusch-Pagan rejects homoskedasticity in all four case models (p = 9.0×10−11, 1.8×10−5, 1.1×10−4, 3.4×10−4 for Q1–Q4). Residual kurtosis runs 8.7 to 9.9 in the multivariate models and 106.6 in the simple one — the trophy sale, unabsorbed. Reported standard errors are therefore optimistic at the top of the market in every specification.
